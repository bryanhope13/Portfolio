Inclusive product concept

Data Dignity

Personal data is collected constantly and stored everywhere. This concept gives one person — Jordan, a teen who needs an ADHD-friendly view — a snapshot of where data lives, who can see it, and what to do next without panic.

Role
Product design, UI
Focus
ADHD-friendly privacy
Platform
Mobile
Methods
8-pt grid, chunking, IA

The problem

Privacy products often fail in the same way: they show everything. Permissions lists, tracker logs, and security alerts compete until the user either ignores the app or feels worse than before they opened it. For someone with ADHD, that wall of equal-weight information is not just ugly — it is unusable.

Jordan still needs the truth: what is public, what is shared with adults, what changed this week, which high-risk permissions are still on. The job was to make that truth scannable in a few seconds, then point to one next action.

Design priorities

I treated cognitive load as the primary constraint, not a style preference. Cards are chunked. Spacing is consistent on an 8-point grid. Status language is specific (“review needed,” “getting safer”) instead of alarmist. The dashboard answers three questions in order: where is the data, who can see it, and what changed.

Decisions I would defend in a critique

  1. 01
    Snapshot over settings.

    The home view is an overview, not a control panel. Storage, permissions, and activity sit as separate cards so Jordan can finish one thought at a time.

  2. 02
    Metrics that imply a next step.

    “3 / 5 high-risk permissions still on” is more useful than a generic security score. The number is a prompt, not a grade.

  3. 03
    Calm, not cute.

    Rounded cards and a muted palette lower threat. Color is reserved for status. The lock mark is a promise of control, not a warning siren.

  4. 04
    Three destinations, not a maze.

    Dashboard, Data, Settings. If a privacy app needs a hamburger of twelve items, the information architecture is already lost.

What I’d do next

This is a concept, not a shipped product. The next honest step is testing with teens — including people who use ADHD accommodations — on two tasks: “What needs your attention today?” and “Turn off something that feels too public.” I would also design the empty and error states; privacy UIs are full of both.
